Challenge Overview 
Design an AI-powered assistant that helps customers navigate storm-related outage 
uncertainty. 
The Challenge 
A major storm is approaching. Customers need timely, personalized, and understandable answers before an 
outage occurs and throughout restoration. Your team will build an AI-powered customer assistant that combines 
predictive analytics, geospatial context, network relationships, operational restoration inputs, and customer 
communication design. 
 
The customer should be able to ask: 
• “Will I lose power?”   
• “How long could restoration take?”   
• “What should I do to prepare?”   
• “Do I qualify for outage alerts?” 
 
Your Mission 
●​
Estimate the likelihood of an outage for an anonymized premise, circuit, or location. 
●​
Estimate a credible restoration time range using known or simulated damage and operational conditions. 
●​
Provide clear preparation and safety recommendations tailored to the customer's context. 
●​
Determine alert eligibility and recommend proactive communication and escalation triggers. 
●​
Explain the key factors behind every prediction without exposing sensitive operational or customer data. 
Why This Challenge Matters 
Value 
What success looks like 
Customer impact 
Customers receive useful guidance before, during, and after a storm. 
Utility modernization 
Teams connect AI, geospatial analysis, network topology, and restoration 
operations. 
Operational realism 
Recommendations reflect damage types, dependencies, labor, travel, and 
restoration sequencing. 
Responsible AI 
Predictions are transparent, privacy-conscious, safe, and appropriately uncertain. 
 
Intended Users 
Primary users are residential customers. Teams may also support anonymized priority locations such as 
life-support customers, water-treatment facilities, hospitals, schools, or other critical loads, provided the 
experience protects identity and avoids revealing sensitive locations.

Challenge Requirements 
Build a working experience that demonstrates prediction, restoration reasoning, 
recommendation quality, and proactive communication. 
1. Outage Risk Prediction 
●​
Accept an anonymized premise, circuit, or map location as the customer context. 
●​
Produce an outage-risk estimate or clearly defined risk band. 
●​
Use relevant signals such as historical damage patterns, asset type, circuit or protective-device relationships, 
geospatial proximity, and available storm characteristics. 
●​
Show the most important contributing factors and communicate uncertainty. 
2. Restoration-Time Estimate 
●​
Provide an estimated restoration duration or range rather than a false-precision promise. 
●​
Consider damage type and quantity, upstream dependencies, step-restoration opportunities, labor-hour 
assumptions, travel, and nonproductive time. 
●​
Update the estimate when damage, crew, access, or network assumptions change. 
●​
Distinguish model output from confirmed utility restoration information. 
3. Personalized Preparation Guidance 
●​
Provide customer-friendly actions appropriate to the predicted risk and expected duration. 
●​
Include safety, practical preparation guidance and clearly label emergencies or safety messages. 
●​
Adapt recommendations for declared accessibility, medical, or critical-facility needs without inferring sensitive 
attributes. 
4. Alerts and Proactive Communications 
●​
Determine whether the scenario qualifies for outage-related alerts using documented rules or challenge 
assumptions. 
●​
Recommend notification timing, channel, and escalation triggers. 
●​
Generate concise, plain-language messages for pre-storm, outage-confirmed, estimate-updated, and 
restoration-complete scenarios. 
5. Explainability and Responsible Use 
●​
Make the reason for each recommendation understandable to a nontechnical customer. 
●​
Disclose confidence, major assumptions, missing data, and conditions that could change the answer. 
●​
Avoid exposing raw personal information, employee contact details, precise sensitive-facility locations, or 
internal-only operational fields. 
●​
Include a clear fallback when the model cannot make a reliable prediction.

Solution Expectations 
Teams may choose their own architecture and modeling approach. The submission must make 
the end-of-the-end customer journey clear. 
Minimum Viable Demonstration 
1.​ A customer selects or enters an anonymized location. 
2.​ The assistant returns an outage-risk prediction with a confidence indicator and explanation. 
3.​ The customer explores damage or restoration scenarios and receives an estimated restoration range. 
4.​ The assistant recommends preparation actions and indicates alert eligibility. 
5.​ The solution generates at least one proactive customer communication. 
6.​ The team shows how privacy, safety, uncertainty, and model limitations are handled. 
Suggested Technical Components 
Layer 
Examples of acceptable approaches 
Experience 
Conversational interface, web or mobile prototype, map-based journey, 
accessible visualization. 
Data 
Curated tabular extract, geospatial layers, topology graph, and synthetic 
customer profiles were needed. 
Analytics 
Classification or risk scoring, regression or survival-style duration estimate, 
rules, graph analysis, geospatial features. 
AI assistant 
Retrieval and orchestration, natural-language explanation, scenario reasoning, 
communication generation. 
Controls 
Prompt and input validation, confidence thresholds, human escalation, privacy 
filters, audit trail. 
 
Recommended Output Design 
●​
Use a risk band and confidence level that are easy to understand. 
●​
Present restoration as a range with the factors most likely to move it. 
●​
Separate confirmed facts, model estimates, and customer actions. 
●​
Use plain language and accessible interaction patterns. 
●​
Allow judges to change at least one scenario input and observe the response. 
 
Important 
Teams are not expected to reproduce a production outage-management system. A focused, credible 
prototype with transparent assumptions is preferred over broad functionality that cannot be 
explained.
